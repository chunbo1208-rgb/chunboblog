Perhaps you have found yourself in this situation:

> “I have spent so much time on this, but I still cannot see the results. I have already invested my time and energy, yet I have felt stuck for a long time. Should I quit?”

Many successful people tell us that persistence is the key to success: never give up. They compare progress to heating water. It may not boil at 99°C, but one more degree can make all the difference.

It is an appealing image. But how do we know that we are actually approaching the boiling point? How do we distinguish persistence from waiting indefinitely for a breakthrough that may never come?

We naturally love stories of comebacks: someone loses everything, keeps fighting, and eventually returns to the top. Yet some stories end in disappointment. Luck does not arrive for everyone, and effort does not guarantee the outcome we want.

That is why we need to learn how to quit, too. Sometimes, giving up on one path is part of a larger strategy. We need to step back and consider whether the direction still serves the life we want to build.

## The Pain of Letting Go

Why do people keep betting even after they have lost so much? Why might a student continue preparing for a competition with only a few remaining chances, even after losing the enthusiasm that brought them to it?

Quitting can feel like cutting off a hand trapped beneath a rock. Leaving offers freedom, but the immediate pain makes it difficult to act. We may know that we want to leave and still struggle to accept what leaving means.

“I have already spent so much” keeps echoing in our minds. It tells us to wait a little longer, to give ourselves one more chance. Sometimes we keep waiting until we have little energy left for anything else.

I know this feeling from my own experience with the Mathematical Olympiad.

I spent more than two years preparing for it. Over time, I began to lose my original enthusiasm and came to dislike the experience of competing. There were several reasons. I felt confused when students who had trained for less time seemed more capable of solving problems. I felt heartbroken when I could not solve an easy problem during an important class.

I began to doubt myself. Perhaps I was not particularly good at mathematical competitions. Perhaps choosing this path had been a mistake from the beginning.

But I kept going. I thought about the money already spent on training, and about the expectations of my parents and teachers. Those thoughts made leaving feel almost impossible. Eventually, I lost so much energy that I felt I had to leave.

Quitting is easier said than done. The decision is shaped not only by our own wishes, but also by our commitments and the people around us.

## Rethinking How We Evaluate Our Choices

To understand the problem, we need to examine how we judge our decisions. Difficulty quitting does not necessarily mean that we lack courage. It may also mean that we are using the wrong criteria to decide whether to continue.

We can think of our choices as investments. When we study, we invest time, energy, and sometimes money in knowledge or future opportunities. When we work, we may invest time in exchange for income, skills, or recognition. The returns are different, but we usually hope to gain something we value.

The difficulty is deciding which returns matter. Experience can be valuable, but “at least I gained experience” does not automatically justify every investment. We need to ask whether that experience is worth the cost, and whether continuing is likely to offer enough value to us.

We also need to distinguish what we have already spent from what we are deciding to spend next. Past effort explains why leaving hurts. By itself, it does not tell us whether continuing is a good choice.

The question therefore becomes: “Given what I know now, is this still worth my next investment of time and energy?”

## Turning the Question into a Decision

Knowing that past effort cannot justify future effort is one thing. Deciding what to do next is harder. I am still working through that uncertainty myself, so I cannot offer a formula that guarantees the right answer. But we can make the decision more concrete.

First, name what you still want from the activity. Is it a particular result, a skill, enjoyment, or an opportunity? “I have already spent two years on this” describes your past; it does not identify a reason to spend another month.

Second, choose a limited period in which to test whether continuing serves that goal. Set a review date and decide beforehand what evidence you will look for. Depending on the activity, that might mean completing a project, solving previously inaccessible problems, or discovering whether a different routine makes the work sustainable. The period should be long enough for meaningful progress to be possible, with a limit on how much time, money, and energy you are willing to spend.

Third, compare continuing with a specific alternative. What would you actually do with the time you regain? A different path has costs and uncertainties too. Compare realistic options, rather than your present difficulties with an imagined perfect future.

Finally, at the review date, choose whether to continue, change your approach, pause, or stop. Missing a checkpoint does not automatically mean quitting: your method or expectations may need revision. But extending the trial should require a clear reason and another review date. Otherwise, “one more chance” can become an endless postponement.

In my own case, I need to distinguish wanting to understand mathematics from wanting to succeed in mathematical competitions. Leaving the competition path does not require abandoning mathematics. That distinction gives me a more precise question: which form of studying mathematics is worth my next investment of time and energy?

We may still feel grief after making a reasonable decision. That feeling alone does not prove the decision was wrong. Learning when to quit is part of learning where to direct our effort.
